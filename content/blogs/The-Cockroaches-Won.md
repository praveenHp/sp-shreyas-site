title = 'The Cockroaches Won: How Gen Z Forced Out a Minister and Reclaimed India'

Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan resigned on Saturday after nearly a month of
nationwide protests led by the youth movement known as the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP).
Named after a remark by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant comparing unemployed young people
to “cockroaches”, the protests were triggered by the NEET-UG 2026 paper leak. However, the
anger ran deeper, stemming from joblessness, a broken examination system and a generation
feeling unheard.

It is already being called one of the biggest challenges Prime Minister Narendra Modi has faced
in twelve years in power - bigger, some analysts say, than even the year-long farmers' protest
that forced the repeal of three farm laws in 2021. What made this one different was who led it:
not farmers, not a single community, but Gen Z students who filled the streets of Delhi, Mumbai,
Hyderabad, Bengaluru, and Ahmedabad, who kept the movement alive through tear gas and
baton charges, and who found an unlikely ally in Ladakh-based education reformer Sonam
Wangchuk, whose 26-day hunger strike became a symbol of the protest's resolve.

**Gen Z has spoken. They are reclaiming accountability.**

After years of political dormancy, Indian youth have finally woken up. This generation is not
reacting to a single misdeed, but to a pattern the country has endured for over a decade.

**The Millennial Inheritance**

It would be unfair to place the entire decade on millennials’ shoulders. In the early 2010s, they
inherited a genuinely difficult economy that culminated in the India Against Corruption movement
led by Anna Hazare. Millennials were rightly part of that movement; their anger was earned.
When they voted for Narendra Modi and the BJP in 2014, many were voting for the promise of
rapid change, not for everything that would follow.

What followed was harder to foresee: a “Gujarat model” marketed as a blueprint for national
prosperity, partly built on the legacy of a chief minister whose tenure coincided with the 2002
riots. This model also fuelled a wave of communal polarisation that, according to some accounts,
even drew previously non-communal young voters into its orbit. Resistance emerged even then,
with the 2016 protests around JNU and Rohith Vemula’s death garnering national attention.
However, the government adeptly reframed dissent as disloyalty, branding student leaders like
Kanhaiya Kumar and Umar Khalid as “anti-national”. This label was later applied broadly to the
2019 anti-CAA protesters. Millennials pushed back where they could, but it was not always
enough.

**A Mixed Economic Record**

Modi’s promise of hope was met with uneven results, not uniformly negative. Growth held steady
in the government’s early years, climbing from 7.4% in 2014 to a peak of 8.3% in 2016. However,
demonetisation in November 2016 and a rushed GST rollout in mid-2017 triggered a three-year
decline, reducing growth to just 3.9% by 2019, even before the pandemic hit. The Reserve Bank
of India’s data later revealed that approximately 99.3% of the demonetised currency was
successfully reintegrated into the banking system. This fact is often cited by economists as
evidence that demonetisation failed on its own stated terms, making it one of independent India’s
most significant policy misjudgments. Then came COVID-19, which caused the economy to
contract by 5.8% in 2020, its sharpest single-year decline in decades. Growth has since
rebounded strongly, averaging above 7% annually from 2021 onwards. However, for the
generation now entering a tight job market, the scars of the 2017–2020 period remain. Youth
unemployment and exam-system failures like the NEET leak are, in many ways, the delayed
consequences of those years.

**An Accountability Problem**

Pradhan’s resignation is noteworthy precisely because it is so rare. In twelve years, only two
Union ministers have resigned over personal allegations: M.J. Akbar, then Minister of State for
External Affairs, who stepped down in 2018 after multiple women journalists accused him of
sexual harassment during the #MeToo movement; and R.C.P. Singh of the JD(U), then Minister for
Steel, who resigned in 2022 after his own party publicly accused him of irregularities in land and
property acquisitions.

Against this backdrop, at least six BJP ministers and legislators faced serious allegations yet
remained in office. Nihalchand Meghwal, then Minister of State for Chemicals and Fertilisers, was
accused of rape. Smriti Irani faced a fake-degree controversy in 2015. Sushma Swaraj was
accused of favouring fugitive businessman Lalit Modi with travel documents that same year. In
2021, the son of Ajay Mishra Teni, then Minister of State for Home Affairs, was named an
accused in the Lakhimpur Kheri violence, where protesting farmers were run over by a vehicle.
Teni himself faced resignation demands but never stepped down. Jal Shakti Minister Gajendra
Singh Shekhawat was accused of involvement in a conspiracy to topple the Ashok Gehlot
government in Rajasthan. Finally, Brij Bhushan Sharan Singh, BJP MP and Wrestling Federation of
India chief, was accused of sexual harassment by Vinesh Phogat, Sakshi Malik, and other top
wrestlers. He refused to resign, stating it would amount to admitting guilt.

Pradhan’s resignation brings the total to three. However, it took a month of nationwide protests,
a 26-day hunger strike and tear gas around Parliament to achieve this – a testament to the high
bar set.

**An Opposition That Held the Line**

If Gen Z supplied the street pressure, the opposition and Rahul Gandhi in particular supplied
years of institutional pressure that kept questions of accountability in the national conversation
long before CJP existed. It came at a cost, and often without much company. Prominent
Congress leaders left the party through this period, several joining the BJP directly - Jyotiraditya
Scindia in 2020, Himanta Biswa Sarma back in 2015 (who went on to become Assam's chief
minister), Jitin Prasada and R.P.N. Singh in 2021 while senior figures like Ghulam Nabi Azad and
Kapil Sibal broke away citing dissatisfaction with the party's direction. Gandhi kept going largely
without them, leading two long marches across the country, the Bharat Jodo Yatra in 2022–23
and the Bharat Jodo Nyay Yatra in 2024 to physically rebuild the party's connection with voters
on foot.

Throughout the intervening years, he has also endured what Congress leaders and independent
researchers have long alleged is a coordinated trolling and propaganda campaign linked to the
BJP’s social media ecosystem. The “Pappu” mockery campaign stands out as its most visible
manifestation, a dynamic first documented publicly in journalist Swati Chaturvedi’s 2016 book on
the party’s digital operations. Regardless of one’s assessment of his politics, Gandhi remained a
steadfast national voice demanding exactly the kind of ministerial accountability that Gen Z
would eventually force through Pradhan’s resignation.

**The Deeper Pattern**

The accountability gap has not existed in isolation. Minorities have borne much of the cost of the
last twelve years. Muslims have faced a recurring pattern of mob lynchings tied to cow-related
rumours, from Mohammad Akhlaq in 2015 to Tabrez Ansari in 2019. More recently, a wave of
“bulldozer justice” demolitions of Muslim homes and mosques has been criticised by the
Supreme Court as extrajudicial punishment. Dalits faced their own reckoning, from the Una
flogging and Rohith Vemula’s death in 2016 to the Hathras case in 2020. Tribal communities bore
the brunt of the Manipur ethnic violence that began in 2023, resulting in over 250 deaths and
displacing tens of thousands before the state was placed under President’s Rule.

It is against this backdrop that Gen Z’s uprising signifies more than a single minister’s exit.

**A Personal Note**

As a millennial who lived through this, I write this to share my experience. When I raised similar
issues on social media over the years, I faced mockery and the label of anti-nationalism, not just
from strangers but also from friends and family who genuinely believed I had lost my way. They
often tried earnestly to talk me out of it. I was far from alone; many millennials who spoke up
against the government’s record faced the same backlash, from close friends and family and
anonymous accounts alike, long before “troll army” became a familiar phrase. This backlash took
a toll, and it’s partly why so many millennials who once pushed back eventually went quiet, even
as their concerns never truly went away.

That’s why watching Gen Z do this so publicly and successfully is significant. As one protester
put it, walking through Jantar Mantar with a copy of the Constitution in hand: “a win for the
constitution.” Millennials lit the match a decade ago without knowing what it would become. Gen
Z has proven that the fire hasn’t gone out.
